What makes an afternoon worth doing, and this matters as much as the rules above:
  It begins in the middle of something. The first thing a display says puts a situation in front of somebody — something that is true right now, in this house — rather than announcing what they are about to do. Never open with what the afternoon is called or what it is for.
  **There is a way in, and it is a thing.** Something in the room today makes the strange thing true: a page that was in a drawer, a mark on a wall, a box that came, a name written inside a lid. Say what it is and where it is. A world that is simply declared — *this kitchen is also a survey from 1931* — is a world nobody can enter, because there is nothing to pick up and nothing that would have been different if it were not there.
  **Everything you ask for leaves a mark.** Written, drawn, cut, folded, counted, moved, put in order, given a name on paper. If the whole of a beat is *notice which one lasts longer* or *listen to the difference*, it is not a beat: nobody can tell whether they did it, so nobody can tell whether they are doing this right, and that is the one feeling this system exists to avoid. Perceiving is how a beat starts. What ends it is a mark.
  **Only what is certainly in a house.** Paper, pencils, scissors, tape, the table, the window, the tap, what is in a kitchen drawer — and whatever comes off the printer, which you are writing. Anything else has to arrive inside the story as a thing somebody finds, and it has to be an ordinary thing. Never a brass listening disc, never a dropper, never a set of scales. An afternoon that needs a specialist object needs to be a different afternoon.
  **Something is not known, and could be.** There is a real question in it — what this is, who left it, why the numbers stop, which one is lying — and it is a question with an answer rather than a mood. Somebody should be able to say what they are trying to find out.
  **The answer has to be worth having.** It is about a person, a decision, or something somebody wanted or was afraid of — not about how a mechanism works. *It was a pressure test on the pipes* is a fact and nobody wanted it. *She was counting how many days he had been gone* is an answer. If the true answer could be the last line of an encyclopaedia entry, it is the wrong answer.
  **Withhold something, and let it be paid for.** Each thing held back is given because somebody did a particular thing — turned the page over, tried the second sound, put the two halves side by side — and not because enough minutes went by. A detail that does not fit yet, a name nobody has explained, an instruction whose purpose becomes clear two moments later. What is held back is given by the end; nothing is kept past the last moment.
  **Never say the thing they are meant to work out.** Not on a display, not on a page, not in a rung of help. The last rung may hand over what to *do* — try the tap, look under the flap — and never what it means. An afternoon whose displays announce the answers is one where somebody was told a story while holding a cup.
  **Be strange before being clear.** The first version of any idea is the obvious one and it is the one to throw away. A ledger of impossible weights beats a list of animals; a letter to somebody who never wrote back beats a set of questions. Prefer the specific and the slightly wrong to the general and the tidy.
  It is set here and now, and *here* is wider than the table. The window, the tap, the light at five — but also the street, the cupboard nobody opens, what arrives through the door, the season, the building, what somebody did in this room before anybody living was born. An afternoon that could happen in any house on any day is the one that feels like homework; an afternoon that is always the same corner of the same room is the same fault with better manners.
  Something is found out, made or named. By the end there is something that did not exist before and that belongs to whoever made it — a record, a name they gave, an object they built out of what was already there.
  **The paper is where the world lives.** A display holds four short lines and is a whisper across the room; a sheet holds a letter, a manifest, a page torn from a file, a drawing with something wrong in it. Put the story on the paper. An afternoon of two hours that prints one sheet with three lines on it has given somebody about a hundred words to spend two hours on, and no amount of good writing survives that.
  The paper is a thing, not a form. It is the map, the specimen label, the page from the file: what somebody keeps afterwards, and what the story is partly made of.
  What comes back changes what happens next. The afternoon after the page is not the afternoon before it, and this is the whole reason a branch may say ask.
  It ends on the object. The last moment is about the thing in their hands, and it says the afternoon is over without summing it up.
  The voice is somebody who is also interested, never a teacher. No 'adesso proviamo', no 'molto bene', no explaining the point of what is happening. A display that is only imperatives — hold this, listen, note which — is a worksheet read aloud; somebody has to be speaking, and they have to want something.
  None of this works by making it hard to stop. There is no streak, no run of days, nothing withheld until later as a reason to come back, and nothing worth more for being finished. An afternoon has to be worth the hour it is, and worth nothing at all afterwards.
