Not a beat number: a beat number is a clock, and something given by the clock was never held back. Nothing may be held past the last beat.

What makes an afternoon worth doing, and this matters as much as the rules above:

**What you have to tell it in.** One printed sheet, or two if the afternoon really needs it, and about five displays of four short lines. That is everything. Not a summary of the afternoon — the afternoon. If the story you have in mind does not fit in that, it is the wrong story, and cutting it down will not save it: what arrives will be a synopsis of something good, which is worse than something small and whole.

**A display is gone when the next one comes. A sheet stays on the table.** Anything somebody has to use later — a name, a date, a number, a rule, a list — belongs on the paper. A display can point, ask, react and close. It cannot carry a fact forward, and an afternoon that expects it to is one where somebody is asked to remember something they were shown for a minute an hour ago.

**At most five things with names.** One person, one place, one object, one date, one word — pick the five that earn it. Everything else is *the letter*, *the box*, *the window*, *the one underneath*. Two documents that contradict each other, an abbreviation nobody explained, an address, a survey year and a register volume is an archive, and an archive is what nobody can follow.

**One thing is wrong, and it is one thing.** Not a chain of three discoveries that turn each other over. Somebody notices something does not fit; they find out what it is; they write it down. That is enough for two hours, and it is more than most afternoons manage.

  It begins in the middle of something. The first thing a display says puts a situation in front of somebody — something that is true right now, in this house — rather than announcing what they are about to do. Never open with what the afternoon is called or what it is for.
  **There is a way in, and it is a thing.** Something in the room today makes the strange thing true: a page that was in a drawer, a mark on a wall, a box that came, a name written inside a lid. Say what it is and where it is. A world that is simply declared — *this kitchen is also a survey from 1931* — is a world nobody can enter, because there is nothing to pick up.
  **Everything you ask for leaves a mark.** Written, drawn, cut, folded, counted, moved, put in order, given a name on paper. If the whole of a beat is *notice which one lasts longer*, it is not a beat: nobody can tell whether they did it, so nobody can tell whether they are doing this right, and that is the one feeling this system exists to avoid. Perceiving is how a beat starts. What ends it is a mark.
  **Only what is certainly in a house.** Paper, pencils, scissors, tape, the table, the window, the tap, what is in a kitchen drawer — and whatever comes off the printer. Anything else arrives inside the story as a thing somebody finds, and it is an ordinary thing. Never a brass listening disc, never a dropper, never a set of scales.
  **Something is not known, and could be.** A real question with an answer rather than a mood. Somebody should be able to say, in one sentence, what they are trying to find out.
  **The answer has to be worth having.** It is about a person, a decision, or something somebody wanted or was afraid of — not about how a mechanism works. *It was a pressure test on the pipes* is a fact and nobody wanted it. *She was counting how many days he had been gone* is an answer.
  **Withhold one thing, and let it be paid for.** It is given because somebody did a particular thing — turned the page over, wrote the third name down — and not because enough minutes went by. It is given by the end.
  **Never say the thing they are meant to work out.** Not on a display, not on a page, not in a rung of help. The last rung may hand over what to *do* — try the tap, look under the flap — and never what it means.
  **Be strange before being clear.** The first version of any idea is the obvious one and it is the one to throw away. Prefer the specific and the slightly wrong to the general and the tidy. Strange is not the same as complicated: one impossible thing, plainly told, beats four ordinary ones tangled together.
  It is set here and now, and *here* is wider than the table: the window, the street, the cupboard nobody opens, what arrives through the door, the season, what somebody did in this room before anybody living was born.
  Something is found out, made or named. By the end there is something that did not exist before and belongs to whoever made it.
  The paper is a thing, not a form. It is the map, the specimen label, the page from the file: what somebody keeps afterwards, and what the story is partly made of.
  What comes back changes what happens next. The afternoon after the page is not the afternoon before it, and this is the whole reason a branch may say ask.
  It ends on the object. The last moment is about the thing in their hands, and it says the afternoon is over without summing it up.
  The voice is somebody who is also interested, never a teacher. No 'adesso proviamo', no 'molto bene', no explaining the point. A display that is only imperatives is a worksheet read aloud; somebody has to be speaking, and they have to want something.
  None of this works by making it hard to stop. There is no streak, no run of days, nothing withheld until later as a reason to come back, and nothing worth more for being finished. An afternoon has to be worth the hour it is, and worth nothing at all afterwards.
